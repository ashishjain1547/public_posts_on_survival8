Choose Yourself First: The Overlooked Art of Staying Happy

Most of us spend our entire lives trying to make others happy. Parents want their children to smile. Spouses want their partners to feel loved. Business owners want their customers to be satisfied. Society keeps pushing us to serve everyone around us. But in this endless race to please the world, one person gets completely forgotten, you. A spiritual teacher once shared a simple but life-changing truth: if you cannot keep yourself happy, you cannot keep anyone else happy. This post unpacks that idea step by step.

The Silent Sacrifice We Celebrate

Think about a typical Indian housewife. She wakes up early in the morning. She makes tiffin for the children. She prepares breakfast for her husband before he leaves for office. Then she cooks lunch. Guests arrive, so she serves them first. By the time everyone has eaten, the last child gets only two dry rotis, no vegetable, no curry. The mother herself may stay hungry. We often praise this as sacrifice and devotion. But the teacher calls it ignorance, not wisdom. If you feed every guest but remain hungry yourself, you have not done true hospitality. If you keep the whole world happy but remain sad inside, you are not a true yogi. You have simply forgotten your own existence.

An Empty Cup Cannot Pour

There is an old Sanskrit saying, "Dadatu yat yasya asti." It means roughly: a person gives only what he or she already has. If your heart is full of joy, you will naturally give joy to others. If your heart is full of sadness, you will spread sadness without even trying. The same rule applies to your home. If you cook good food in your kitchen, that is what you serve to your guests. If your inner kitchen is empty, you have nothing to offer. So keeping yourself happy is not selfish. It is the first step toward becoming useful to anyone else.

When Expectations Steal the Present Moment

The teacher tells two short stories to show how expectations create suffering. In the first story, a businessman bought a plot of land just outside the city limits. The price was low because the area was not yet under the municipal corporation. Someone told him that within a year the area would be included and the rate would jump. But the government changed, plans changed, and five years passed. The plot did not get included. The rate still increased, but not as much as he had hoped. He became sad, not because he made no profit, but because he expected more. In the second story, a man bought a large piece of land for ten lakh rupees. He sold it for twenty-two lakh rupees. He was happy because his money had more than doubled. But three days later, the same land was resold for forty-four lakh. A week later, it went for sixty-six lakh. Now the first man forgot his profit and began to suffer. The table below shows the pattern clearly.

In both cases, the person gained something real. But expectation made them feel like losers. The teacher says: excessive expectations are the factory of sadness.

The Guru and God Trap

The worst form of expectation happens when people expect too much from their Guru or from God. When you place demands on the divine, you get only what your effort and your destiny can carry. The teacher warns that even among the Jain Tirthankaras there are differences in posture, name, and form. If even gods are not equal, why do we expect equality among human beings? Do your work with full effort, accept the result that comes, and move forward. Remember the golden rule: nobody gets anything before time, and nobody gets more than fate allows. After honest effort, whatever result arrives is exactly what you deserve.

Big Drum, Big Hollow

People often think that rich and powerful people must be very happy. The teacher gives a striking example. He once met the manager of one of the world's largest casinos, a man who handled crores of rupees daily. Yet that man was deeply unhappy. The teacher also points to the prime minister. He has to face public protests, abusive words from opposition parties, and constant pressure from his own party members. A big drum has a big hollow. Bigger position means bigger pain. Happiness is not created by wealth, power, or fame. It is created by how you feel inside.

Two Words That Solve Most Problems

The teacher offers two simple words from Hindi: sadbhav and abhav. Sadbhav means presence, the feeling that something good exists with you right now. Abhav means absence, the feeling that something is missing. Most of us see only abhav. We focus on what we do not have. The teacher says: in presence, learn to see absence before it arrives. In absence, learn to see presence somewhere else. When a loved one dies, we see only absence. But that soul exists somewhere, and the people still with you are a presence you often ignore. Another powerful line from the talk is this: "What you have received is enough." If you can truly accept that one sentence, half of your mental suffering will disappear.

The Daily Promise

The teacher shares a personal practice. Whenever he makes a mistake, he does not become angry with himself. He simply says, "Let it go. This match is over. We will see the next match." Small self-resentments are silent killers of happiness. Many people cannot forgive themselves for tiny errors. This self-punishment destroys their inner peace. Start your day with a simple promise: "I will not be unhappy. I will do my best. I will accept the result. I will not be angry with myself." If you keep this promise, you become truly strong. The teacher ends with a hard but honest statement: "If you cannot keep yourself happy, you are the most unsuccessful and smallest person in the world. And if you cannot keep yourself happy, how will you ever keep anyone else happy?"

A Simple Definition of Success

True success is not about how much you own. It is about whether you can enjoy what you already have. Work hard, pray, be patient, but never let expectations poison the present moment. Keep your own cup full first. Then the overflow will automatically reach everyone around you. That is the quiet art of choosing yourself first, and it is the foundation of all real happiness.
